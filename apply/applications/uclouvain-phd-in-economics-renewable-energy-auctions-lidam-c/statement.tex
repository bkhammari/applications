I am applying for the doctoral position on auction-based support schemes for renewable energy in Europe. This statement sets out the training I would bring to it and the training I would have to acquire.

\sectionrule{Background in energy}

My energy training is at Cologne, where the specialisation is taught by the Institute of Energy Economics. I took \textit{Energy Markets and Regulation} with Jun.-Prof.\ Dr.\ Oliver Ruhnau, whose chair is in energy market design, and finished it with 1.3 on a scale where 1.0 is the maximum, followed by \textit{Growth, Energy and Climate Change} with PD Dr.\ Dietmar Lindenberger.

My final seminar paper, written this summer in the Panel Data Methods seminar at Cologne, is on carbon leakage from the EU Emissions Trading System. It asks whether increases in the effective EU ETS carbon cost raised EU imports of emission-intensive goods from Morocco, Algeria, Egypt and Tunisia between 2005 and 2021, relative both to less emission-intensive goods and to comparison partners, and it develops and defends the design that would answer that question. Aggregate studies of EU manufacturing trade find no statistically significant leakage effect (Naegele and Zaklan, 2019). My argument is that an aggregate null can conceal leakage concentrated in corridors where regulatory asymmetry is large, transport costs are low, and emission-intensive capacity already exists.

The specification is a triple difference, with exposure varying across products through sectoral emission intensity, over time through the effective carbon cost, and across partners through corridor membership, absorbing product-specific, partner-specific and bilateral shocks through three sets of fixed effects.

One feature of that paper is why I am writing to this project rather than to a general applied-micro post. I define the effective carbon cost as the allowance price attenuated by the sector-year free-allocation share, so a sector receiving full free allocation faces an effective cost of zero whatever the headline EUA price does. The objection is that tradable allowances carry the permit price as marginal opportunity cost, so a lump-sum grant should leave the output margin untouched. EU allocation is not lump-sum in that sense: activity thresholds, closure provisions and partial-cessation rules tie allowances to continued production. The headline price of a climate instrument and the incentive it creates are different objects, and the gap between them is a property of the rules.

That is the same problem an auction for renewable capacity presents. An award price is not the support a project effectively receives once pre-qualification requirements, penalties, completion bonds and local content conditions are priced into the bid. I have already written one paper about measuring the effective stringency of a European climate instrument rather than its nominal one, and I would like the next four years to be about the other one.

\sectionrule{Proposed contribution}

Of the four directions in the call, the two I would want to take up are pre-qualification requirements together with completion bonds, and local content requirements. My reason is specific to the identification problem they present.

Auction design in Europe is not a single policy. Member states adopted auctions at different dates and revised the rules at different dates again: penalty levels, bid bonds, realisation deadlines, technology-specific against technology-neutral lots, and local content conditions have each been introduced and amended on separate national timetables. This is staggered adoption of a treatment whose intensity varies across adopters, and it is the setting in which conventional panel estimators fail quietly.

My master's thesis was built for that setting. It estimated the labour-market effects of a national scholarship programme across 558 regions over 2005 to 2019, using the heterogeneity-robust staggered difference-in-differences estimator of Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) with doubly-robust inference, on a panel of roughly five million linked employer-employee records that I constructed. The doubly-robust estimate is a wage effect of $+3.04$ per cent in low-exposure regions; the two-way fixed effects estimate on the same panel is $-0.65$ per cent. The sign reverses for the reason Goodman-Bacon (2021) identifies: comparisons between later-treated and already-treated units are invalid once effects vary across adoption cohorts. The thesis was graded 1.0.

Auction theory delivers predictions about how pre-qualification and penalty regimes change bidding behaviour and completion rates. Testing them against the European record requires an estimator that survives staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects, and the diagnostics that establish whether it has. That is the part of the project I could do on arrival.

My strongest marks are in econometrics, mathematics for economists and energy regulation, and my microeconomics grade is 3.0, which is average on the German scale. I would begin the theoretical component of this project from a lower base than a candidate trained in industrial organization, and the transcript accompanying this application shows that.

Two things qualify it. Mas-Colell, Whinston and Green and Osborne and Rubinstein are the standard the call names, and reaching that standard is a matter of coursework and reading that a first year is designed to accommodate. The empirical craft is the harder thing to add later, and I have it already.
