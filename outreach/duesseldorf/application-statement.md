**Research interests.**
I work on how different higher education access mechanisms — scholarships, loans, and affirmative-action quotas — affect labor market inequality, and on the econometric methods needed to measure those effects credibly. My tools are difference-in-differences estimation under staggered treatment adoption, heterogeneous treatment effects, and doubly-robust inference. A recurring theme in my work is that standard methods hide policy-relevant variation across demographic groups and regions, and that applying recent estimators changes the conclusions.

My master's thesis (grade: 1.0), written at the University of Cologne, is the first microregion-level causal analysis of Brazil's ProUni scholarship program on formal-sector wages. I built a panel of 558 microregions over 2005–2019 by linking ProUni administrative records, RAIS employer–employee data (about five million observations), and Census demographics through the basedosdados BigQuery interface. Using the Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) staggered difference-in-differences estimator with doubly-robust inference, I found that ProUni's wage effects are non-monotonic — significant in low-exposure regions, near zero at higher intensities — and that gains accrue disproportionately to ethnic minorities. A conventional two-way fixed effects specification reversed the sign of the effect and masked this heterogeneity entirely.

**Proposed PhD.**
I want to extend this into a systematic comparison of the three main access mechanisms: which one most reduces labor market inequality, and for whom. Brazil is my empirical setting because it introduced scholarships (2005), student loans (2010), and quotas (2012) at national scale, which gives the staggered variation and administrative data needed for identification. The questions and methods are designed to transfer to other settings, including Germany, where grants (BAföG, Deutschlandstipendium) and a debate about social quotas raise the same design choices. I have a full three-paper proposal covering comparative returns, outcomes beyond wages (formality, occupational upgrading, job stability), and dose-response estimation.

**Why DGSE.**
Two parts of the DICE faculty connect directly to this work. Hannah Schildberg-Hörisch's research on how socioeconomic status shapes skill formation addresses the upstream side of the inequality my project traces into the labor market; combining the two strengthens the chain from access to outcomes. Jens Südekum's work identifying causal labor market effects from regional variation in policy exposure — including job stability and geographical mobility — shares methods and questions with my own. The DGSE's structured, English-language program and the DICE research environment fit both the empirical and the policy sides of my agenda.
